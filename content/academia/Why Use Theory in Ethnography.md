This essay is motivated by a tension I perceive in ethnographic work: an attempt to bring attention to specific personal experience, while at the same time employing theories and coming to conclusions that are more impersonal and general. This use of and coming to theory is a key part of what makes an ethnography something other than a story. But this is complicated, because there are so many different variations of theory and experience, and so many different ways to put them in the same room. It is for this reason that I have not attempted to come to any throughline conclusions. Instead, this essay puts decolonial conceptions of theory in conversation with four ethnographies. All the ethnographies selected have a political dimension to them (that is a coming to theory) as well as use various theoretical frameworks to understand experience and forward their claims. In doing this, I hope to shed some light on the different ways that theory and experience can be combined and encourage practical ways of thinking about theory that can be useful for ethnographic work.
# Theory and Experience

First, like any good academic, I will briefly introduce my terms, and develop them more with the help of Frantz Fanon and Ngugi Wa Thiong’o. I conceive ‘experience’ as something that is discrete and subjective, essentially data that concerns a single subject, participant, or interlocutor. This experience is often the object of the ethnographer’s fixation, either through participant observation, interviews, or archival research. I conceive ‘theory’ as generalizable ideas and knowledge, stories that present an external standpoint or perspective through which we can make sense of the world. Federal regulations define research as “a systematic investigation … designed to develop or contribute to generalizable knowledge” (Protection of Human Subjects, 2018). In this light, the object of ethnographic *research* is the development of theory. Note that I locate theory as a subset of story, for reasons that will become apparent later.
## The Violence of Theory

In Frantz Fanon’s seminal work *Black Skin, White Masks* (2008), we get a visceral account of the ways in which theory can be alienating. Specifically, he draws on Sartre’s conception of the ‘other,’ which serves to illuminate the conflict between individual consciousness and the alienating presence of others; the other’s gaze transforms subject into object.[^1] Fanon encounters theory in this way, through the violence between his lived experience and the alienating intellectualization of blackness. The way in which his race has been theorized is corrosive to his lived experience. To escape this, he comes to the conclusion that the only way out is to wholeheartedly embrace experience, such that alienating theory can be ignored: "Consciousness committed to experience knows nothing, has to know nothing, of the essence and determination of being” (p. 113). In context, he is answering to what discovering a black civilization in the past would mean. For the purposes of this argument, this can be understood as using historical data to theorize Africans as capable of civility.[^2] While this is an extreme example, as the former half of the 20th century in which Fanon lived was not kind in its interpretation of blackness, it compels us to be cautious with using theory in relation to personal experience. Do we use theory to detract from experience or to make sense of it?
## The Function of Theory

To further address what theory does in practice, I think it is helpful to take another detour to Ngugi’s *Decolonizing the Mind* (2011). Ngugi works from a marxist standpoint to identify two functions of language: first as communication related to material processes of production, and second as a carrier of culture (pp. 13-15). He argues that language carries within it images and values, transferred down through generations of collective struggle, that we base our conceptions of ourselves on. That the extent to which these images line up with reality is the extent to which we can creatively confront our problems and understand ourselves. This has interesting implications for the subject that Ngugi studies: the politics of African language. He posits that colonized African is systematically distanced from their language and have less access to the images it would impart. In fact, with the implementation of the English language in colonies, Africans are transmitted images from the language of their oppressors. This alienation from their language leads to a loss of being able to understand oneself, and instead understand oneself through the eyes of their oppressor. 

Now, in order to connect these two authors' experiences to theory, we must make a jump to see theory as an image itself, a framing that is transmitted through language which we use to understand subjective experience. Fanon encountered the alienation that Ngugi describes when he encountered (French) images that rendered his experience irrational. Although Fanon initially committed himself only to experience in BSWM, he later shifted his position more in line with Ngugi by allowing for the discovery of a black past, as long as it serves to clarify the present and ignite material action (2004, p. 167). To extrapolate, theory is important in that it can clarify our own experience, much like didactic stories can do.  
## The Role of Ethnography 

Since ethnography is characterized by both its experience-gathering methods and its use of theory, it is important to understand the relationship between the two. It may very well be this distinctive combination that separates ethnography from the likes of journalism or some social sciences. How do ethnographies mobilize theory and experience and to what end? How is the author’s political project related to how they assemble theory and experience? Does the use of theory bring attention to experience, or does the discovered experience ground a theory? In a sense, theory is a practical technology in that it regulates how we come to understand experience. It is both a tool in the ethnographer’s toolkit, as well as a strategic appeal to an external standpoint for the purposes of building their authority. It can also be a way to avoid judgements of good and evil while still maintaining a critical position.   

With these questions and frames, I will analyze the ways that *Learning to Labor* by Paul Willis (1981), *Everything was Forever Until It Was No More* by Alexi Yurchak (2006), *Computing Taste* by Nick Seaver (2022), and *The Democracy Project* by David Graber (2013) utilize theory and experience. Again, my intent is not necessarily to come to a general theory or draw straight lines, but to identify the different combinations of theory and experience and what their effects are. To understand what impact the author’s analytical methods have on the articulation (or non-articulation) of their political project.
# Learning To Labor

First we will start with Paul Willis’ work *Learning To Labor* (1981), which seeks to answer the question: how do working class kids get working class jobs? More importantly, why do others let them and why do they let themselves? The main feature of this study is a group of twelve working class boys from a “good” boys school in the center of England, of which Willis conducted extensive interviews and participant observation with. This group was also supplemented with surrounding research, but it is primarily the ‘lads’ lengthy dialogues recounted in verbatim that he works with the most for his analysis. In a sense, this ethnography finds the world in a cup.

This work is interesting for a number of reasons. First, the book is divided into two distinct sections: one titled ‘Ethnography’ and the following titled ‘Analysis.’ The first section is familiar in its ethnographic structure, which presents experience (primarily thick interviews), then comments on the emergent cultural themes and how we must make sense of the data. It works with the experience of the lads in a way that feels sincere and makes us sympathetic to their qualms. The unedited, long, heartfelt interview plays an important role in emotionally capturing us to the side of the lads. Often the majority of the page consists of a transcript, while only a paragraph serves to unpack it. One specific aspect of the interviews that are extensively unpacked is the use of language and vernacular. This further serves to bring us into their world, and understand the content and struggles of their daily lives. At the end of the ethnography component, you are left with a feeling that you understand how the lads see their life. From the point of view that they’ve shown you, the reason they get working class jobs makes sense. The second part is more unfamiliar. It is a farther-removed analysis, and a clearly marxist one. These chapters introduce his own theories, which treat the experience of the lads in an interesting way, and give us some clarity on how Willis thinks about theory.
## Partial Trust

The first three chapters of the analysis lay out Willis’ theories of ‘penetrations,’ ‘limitations,’ and ‘ideology’ which work together to interpret the experience of the lads. Penetrations are defined as impulses the lads have toward understanding and articulating their position within the class system and limitations are the forces that impede a true recognition and expression of these impulses. Willis concedes that ethnography interacts with a situation in which these are both in play, but not separable. Thus, we are left with ‘partial penetrations,’ or the interplay between these two forces that results in the existing culture (p. 119). He places the lads' experience on a continuum from naivety, toward the complete penetration of the exploitative system they are oppressed by, with limitations like ideology that hinder their progress toward a non-individualist structured understanding of society. Ideology here functions to confirm partial penetrations that do not challenge labor extraction, or dislocate these partial penetrations by allowing for new systems that retain some of the original penetration, but still do not challenge systems of production (p. 161). 

The important takeaway here is that, by various mechanisms, these theories serve to partially direct attention away from the individual experiences of the lads, and toward an apprehension of a class system. His theories of penetration and limitation ask us to trust the lads, but not completely—to trust their ‘impulse,’ not their conclusions. This is ultimately how he builds his political project, by attempting to redirect the way we’ve been brought into the lads’ worldview instead towards a marxist one.[^3] The theories he develops do not necessarily clarify the lads experience, they overtly acknowledge its convolutedness, but rather induct experience into a marxist system.
## Willis’ Theory

In the appendix, Willis clearly expresses how he thinks about theory, and in order to unpack it I can see no other way than to first quote it at length:  

>The ethnographic account, for all its faults, records a crucial level of experience and through its very biases insists upon a level of human agency which is persistently overlooked or denied … Although the world is never directly ‘knowable’ … it must nevertheless be specifically registered in theory if theory pretends to be \[of\] any relevance at all. Theories must be judged ultimately for the adequacy they display to the understanding of the phenomenon they purport to explain \- not to themselves. (p. 194\)  

To Willis, if a marxist theory of labor is to be of any relevance, then his ethnographic account must be registered within it. It must be taken, analysed, and placed within its bounds. A key observation that Willis makes in the endnotes for chapter eight is that Marx does not fully answer the question of how labor power is formed and reproduced (p. 179). This is one of the key questions that Willis set out to resolve, and with his theories of penetration and limitation the experience of the lads can fit nicely within a marxist framework. They are molded to provide insight into how labor power in a capitalist society is reproduced and maintained.  

His framing of theory is similar to this paper’s framing of theory. They are both functional technologies that are useful in that they can explain an experience. However I presume that Willis is using the experience of the lads to more fully explain labor power instead of working with it to make sense of the experience in itself. In the sense that ethnography seeks to understand the subjects from *their* point of view, Willis rather seems to bring them under his own umbrella. The theories he develops in his paper are therefore *intermediate* and ultimately lead back to structuralist rendition of the world. He sets out to resolve the contradictions within labor theory, less the contradictions within real experience, which as we will see in the next section, is another possible route.
### Reception

If I had to guess, this is one of the reasons a common critique of the work is it puts people off of theory.[^4] If ethnography is a discipline that seeks to understand someone else’s various interpretations of the world in their own terms, it sort of defeats the point to frame your interlocutors as misinterpreting the world. Instead, it makes more sense to bring your own ideas to build understanding *with* your subject.[^5] Otherwise, ethnographic data is turned into a yardstick to measure your own political project against. It becomes a process of hypothesis testing, often placed in opposition with the more heralded “grounded theory” (Spradley, 1980, p. 15). Willis’ approach gets us closer to the kind of thing that Fanon experienced: an intellectualization of a people in terms unfamiliar to them. Here I am reminded of the lads’ indifference to Willis’ analysis section, one of them even going so far as to claim that he eventually got to dislike him (p. 195). They read their own stories more than his.
# Everything Was Forever, Until It Was No More

Alexei Yurchak’s book *Everything Was Forever Until It Was No More* (2006) has a seemingly related, but ultimately different ethnographic approach. First, It seeks to address a contradiction in *experience*; during the soviet era, society felt stable and like it would last forever, but when it ended, its members felt that it was inevitable and that they were prepared (pp. xii, 1-2). He does so through the eyes of the last soviet generation, using rich ethnographic materials from author-conducted interviews, to film and fiction, to official documents and publications, to personal diaries, drawings, and more—importantly from both contemporaneous and retroactive perspectives (pp. 38-40). The materials and methods he engages with are much more diverse than Willis’, using historical and linguistic analysis in addition to a more traditional ethnographic account.[^6] This makes for an extremely enjoyable read. Second, Yurchak’s political project, which is overtly expressed in the preface, is to re-humanize soviet experience. To move away from the mainstream binary view that soviet life was split between the false public self and an authentic private one (pp. ix, 21-24). These two aspects lead attention to be directed in an interesting way. 

Before we continue, it is worth mentioning that Yurchak himself is part of the last Soviet generation. Born in 1960 in Leningrad, he was a trained physicist and a music group organizer before moving to the US in 1997\. Although both these activities are described in the book, he remains almost uncomfortable using his own voice. In something like a sleight of hand, he claims a “decentered” positionality (by means of US experience) that allows for a sovereign authorial voice (p. 44). And this is the way it reads. Self-reflexivity is replaced by a deeper use of theory to unpack ethnographic materials, and it feels authoritative.
## Constructive Attention

Yurchak works heavily with John Austin’s theory that language contains “constative” utterances as well as “performative” ones. The former states something, and can therefore be true or false, while the latter does something, and can be successful or unsuccessful (pp. 25, 380). He uses this theoretical grounding to urge the reader to pay more attention to what the last generation of Soviets *did*, not necessarily what they *said* (pp. ix-x). That because of certain historical conditions and discursive constraints, the performative dimension of language became more important than the constative dimension. For example, the Soviets voted and participated in state-sponsored organizations because it was *normal* and these acts enabled a creative and meaningful life. What mattered was *successfully* reproducing a social existence.  

Paying attention to Soviet experience in this way makes it legible in a way that is not reductive. It trusts experience. In line with Yurchak’s political project, it steers the reader away from value judgements and a binary conception of Soviet life, while at the same time providing a clarity that does not rely on an external structure. The use of theory is generative, rather than inductive. Yurchak’s focus on performance ostensibly sounds like Willis’ selective attention to the lads’ “impulse,” but in practice it preserves a higher degree of the subject’s autonomy. By allowing them to be correct, his arguments act on our understanding more than their actions; we just need to understand some things as performative instead of evaluating strictly for constative truth—“we can understand in this way” instead of “this is what their actions mean to us.” Anthropology has long been critiqued for evaluating culture through foreign values and epistemology,[^7] and Yurchak’s approach seems to understand this. The difference is subtle, but the implications are significant.
### The Case of Creativity

A good example of this difference can be found in the way that Willis and Yurchak handle the topic of creativity. They both conceive of creativity as something that happens within a social structure that must be reproduced. However, Yurchak departs from Willis in that his creativity serves to enable a fulfilling and moral life within a system that can be otherwise ignored (pp. 35-38, 385-386).[^8] For Willis, when limitations come in contact with penetrations to make partial penetrations, creativity serves as a convoluting force that leads to “profound entrapments barred over more by the flush of subjective certainty” (p. 120). Creativity has a negative connotation instead of a positive one. Again we see that Willis’ account detracts from human agency and plurality, while Yurchak’s use of theory clarifies and humanizes. I am unsure if there is any account of how Yurchak’s work was received by the generation he studied, but if his identity as one of them is of any indication, I would presume it to be positive. Certainly not the kind of othering that Fanon describes.

A caveat here is that the both authors approach their studies in analytically different ways. While Willis narrowly seeks to understand the experience of the working class, Yurchak explicitly does not try to provide a general account of the Soviet experience.[^9] Instead he focuses on societal transformation, which is not unique to a certain social class (p. 43). Because of this, the study is less concerned with its degree of “representedness,” a methodological approach he broadly critiques in the preface (p. x-xii). Indeed, I don’t believe Yurchak would be interested in studying the working class in isolation. It is the marxist that goes straight for what they perceive to be the jugular.
# Computing Taste

Nick Seaver’s *Computing Taste* (2022) is a much different ethnography than the two we have seen so far. Although it echoes Yurchak’s book structure—touching on historical conditions then moving to topical chapters—it approaches a phenomenon that is actively unfolding, as opposed to something that happened in the past. The book is about how people who make music recommender algorithms think about their work.[^10] It is about the durable ways of thinking that influence how these systems are constructed, starting from the point that algorithms are sociotechnical in nature (pp. 6-7). Seaver writes with the undertone that algorithms are primarily a product of these epistemic factors; by studying how people think about taste, we can understand how the algorithms themselves work. That studying these algorithms in isolation is misguided, because these epistemic factors may be coded into the systems in indirect or invisible ways. For example, he points out that literature on interpretability in machine learning rarely contains the word “interpretation” (p. 111). Even if systems are made interpretable, we still have to get to the business of interpreting them.
## Methods and Structure

Part of the reason that Seaver’s book is organized like this is because of methodological challenges. Since this is a work of corporate anthropology[^11] on algorithms, he is presented with the additional challenges of “algorithms as technically closed black boxes, \[and\] corporations as sealed-off parts of society” (p. 15). The former led him to focus on epistemic factors, and the latter inspired him to come up with new ethnographic techniques for gathering experience.[^12] The widely fragmented methods he ended up with, from a three-month internship to informal “frieNDA” gatherings lead the book to be grouped by metaphor (pp. 17-19). This is different from how Yurchak’s chapters are grouped by example. There is less of a coherent theory running through Seaver’s book, so it comes across as almost taxonomic. To be clear, I thoroughly enjoyed reading the book, but his conception of algorithms as an amalgam of different theories of taste, and the fragmented ways he accessed these experiences, don’t lend toward the kind of strike-through theory that Yurchak was able to develop. Instead, we get a number of themes that do a good job defamiliarizing the work of recommender makers and making it interesting, but are hesitant to come to something new.
## Sprinkling in Theory

Just like the way that experience is used, and maybe as a consequence of it, Seaver uses theory in a more fragmented way. There are not central theories that he works with throughout the book like Yurchak or Willis. Seaver uses theory to build up individual metaphors and topical categories, but not to run them together. They craft small stories within an anthology. The most enduring invocation of theory for me is Haraway's view of critical theory: we should be critical not to draw straighter lines, but to investigate how we produce humanness and machineness (p. 7). This sentiment runs through the book and informs how he thinks of the relationship between theory and experience himself, but isn’t worked with to craft a new conclusion.

On the topic of capitalism’s role in recommendation systems, Seaver comes to the conclusion that theory and experience operate at different scales. He concedes that people who make these algorithms may very well be caught up in the reproduction of consumer desire, but that these large-scale dynamics don’t necessarily capture local feelings; the grand theories are not wrong, they just operate at a different level of abstraction (p. 29). This is why he works with the idea of myth, because it is a “scaling device.” For example, identifying “information overload” as a myth places it in a historical context, where it is defamiliarized and challengeable (p. 34). It takes a seemingly neutral present thing and connects it to large-scale dynamics. This is a view of theory that says it does not have to be related to experience. Willis and Yurchak, in a sense, locate large scale dynamics within individual experiences, where Seaver thinks they can exist separately. For Seaver, theory can be used to clarify an experience by drawing them parallel, but it does not have to exist within it.
### Trust

Seaver’s theoretical approach truly does honor the experience of his interlocutors, but he has something final to add regarding trust. In a chapter that works with the metaphor of trapping, he states “one of anthropologists’ many privileges is discursive mobility: we can take our interlocutors more and less literally than they take themselves, pursuing connections that they may not make” (pp. 51-52). In this section, his goal is to place ethnographic data alongside the anthropology of animal trapping which complicates ideas about agency within technical systems. This is one of the ways he creates meaning of his own. Willis trusts his subject’s impulse, Yurchak trusts his subject's performances, and Seaver trusts his subjects to varying degrees of literalness—making theoretical connections by way of more or less literal meaning. These techniques all serve to build a certain kind of trust in the interlocutor, from which an ethnographer might be able to draw their own authority and conclusions from.
# The Democracy Project

The last work I will look at, and a less conventional ethnography at that, is David Graber's *The Democracy Project* (2013). The book relays his personal experience and account of Occupy Wall Street and explores the possibility of democracy in America. It is very different from the other ethnographies discussed up till now for two primary reasons. First is his minimal use of theory. While Yurachek and Seaver have 20 pages of references in addition to their notes, and Willis' notes are theoretically dense as he intentionally removes his specialized arguments to them (p. vii), Graber has no references and minimal notes. The notes in chapter two are mainly articles and news publications, and only chapter three cites a significant amount of academic sources (pp. 305-311). Second is his role in the Occupy movement itself. Graber was no outside observer, but actively engaged in supporting the movement. He was an activist, and his role was to bridge camps. It is because of these two things that the book distinctly does not read like an ethnography—it feels more like something Malcolm Gladwell would write than an academic. Is this an ethnography at all?[^13]

If we don't get too picky with our definitions, I think we'd find it is. Spradley outlined a specific category it would fall under, “complete participation,” where an ethnographer studies a social situation they are actively part of (p. 61\).[^14] Ethnography is primarily concerned with experience and making sense of it from an insider's perspective. As an insider himself, Graber can provide an account of what this "democratic impulse" is like (pp. xvi-xvii). Had another ethnographer, completely unconnected to Occupy, wanted to study it, he would be a great participant. Additionally, Graber uses his experience much like an ethnographic datapoint. He is not attempting to set the record straight on Occupy and provide an authoritative account, but to illustrate what being at the "fulcrum of such a historical convergence" feels like (pp. 4-5). His use of this experience in the book does not require it to be true, it only serves to tell a story about what it was emotionally like to be at Occupy. I suspect an outside ethnographer would use it in the same way. This does however pose an interesting dynamic for trust. 
## Theory and Authority

In the other ethnographies trust is more complex: the authors use theory with external academic authority and the interlocutors are amorphous but interpretable. For these, you just need trust in the job of assembling theory and experience in a coherent way. It feels more like putting a commercial puzzle together than cutting out your own pieces. This, I argue, is an easier task; especially if the theories hail you and the interlocutors are endearing. Authority is dispersed between three entities: the author, the experience (ethnographic data), and the theory. But in Graber's, trust is concentrated in the author. He is more vulnerable in this way. If Graber himself is not convincing, there is no theory or raw data[^15] to save him. This is not to say that the work is only his opinion—he does for example work with history, economic theory, and polling data—just that Graber is the primary figure that brings authority to the work. To accept it, he must be your ethos. However, I think in this specific case this is not so much of an issue and trust is not necessarily needed; his intention in the book is not for you to trust him. It's more likely he simply wants to be provocative.
## Political Project

In many ways, *The Democracy Project* carries a lot of similar tendencies to Occupy itself. Throughout this book Graber wants to ignite your imagination to imagine a world that is more democratic and human (pp. 284-285). The central way he does this is by making legible the contradictions in conventional wisdom. This is why he can simultaneously critique the field of economics, while at the same time use economic arguments to challenge mainstream ideas. The goal is to make the world unfamiliar, so that imagination can step in and make a better one. This is also why he does not make heavy use of academic theory (with the exception of chapter three, a historical grounding). "A real revolution must always operate at the level of common sense" (p. 109). This book operates on similar principles, it's prefigurative in that way. The theories that Graber uses are not the institutionalized and peer-reviewed kind, they are provocative images that he creates or relays, dynamic defamiliarizing devices that are useful in some moments and entirely discardable at others. Very Ngugi-esque.
## Theory's Place

So where do the more academic variations of theory fit into this revolution of common sense? I think we can get an answer from the way that Graber talks about utopian visions. Commenting on a theorist who worked out a plan for how a modern economy could work without money on a participatory basis, Graber says that these are merely thought experiments (pp.282-284); that societies are not created to the shape of someone's blueprints, in reality they are contested and negotiated, with new questions and problems arising at every turn. Utopian visions are useful in that they allow us to talk about possibilities. However, they have limits and are merely objects of our imagination to play with and to use. This is why I think that it is a mistake to try to evaluate this as if it is academic work. It’s an ethnography written by an anthropologist. And its goal is to not to build enduring blueprints, but to corrode the unhelpful ones in conventional wisdom by foul means or fair. To bring it back to Fanon, a “bourgeois society is any society that becomes ossified in a predetermined mold, stifling any development, progress, or discovery … and a man who takes a stand against this living death is in a way a revolutionary” (2008, p. 199).
# Conclusion

In this paper I have attempted to show different ways that theory and experience can be combined, using four ethnographies as points of inquiry and a working definition of theory built from the works of Frantz Fanon and Ngugi wa Thiong’o. In doing so, I have outlined how theory can be thought of as either taking attention away from experience, using experience to be generative, or simply existing at a level separate from experience. I’ve also touched on how an author’s political project influences their use of theory, and more briefly on how an author derives authority from theory and experience. This essay is intended to assist in writing ethnography by providing case studies that inspire different ways of mobilizing theory and experience for some end. It is intended to ignite the ethnographer's imagination, and to encourage ways of writing that honor experience and use theory in strategic, generative, non-reductive ways. “There should be no attempt to fixate man, since it is his destiny to be unleashed” (Fanon, 2008, p. 205).
